# Planning: steps 1 to 5

## Step 1: Assignment

Instruction "look into the Schlerg acquisition" -> write "Should we acquire
the Schlerg Company?" and answer that, never a survey of the company.

State an approved problem statement in one of three forms:

1. As a question: "What should we do to ...?"
2. As a statement of need or purpose: "The base needs to be able to detect
   ..."
3. As an infinitive phrase: "To detect and end the causes of ground mishaps
   ..."

Narrow it until it is usable. "To reduce the crime rate on base" is too
broad; "To detect and end the causes of amphetamine and barbiturate use among
the junior Airmen at Wright-Patterson AFB" is usable.

Solve the root cause, not the symptom. Before drafting the statement, compare
the current situation to the desired end state, define the boundaries, and
answer who, what, when, where, and why the problem affects and occurred.
Asked what to do about falling morale, name the root cause and answer what to
do about it; do not explain how morale fell.

## Step 2: Audience

Omit what the reader already knows: do not define "amortization" for the
company's own CFO; spend the words on the judgment the CFO lacks.

Default reader, unless the assignment says otherwise: intelligent (do not
over-explain), suspicious (every claim needs its support), busy (answer
first, no padding).

The four roles:

- Primary receiver: the person you communicate with directly.
- Secondary receivers: the people the primary receivers pass it on to.
- Key decision makers: the members of the audience who actually decide.
- Gatekeepers: the people who review it before it reaches the intended
  audience.

## Step 4: Thesis

The thesis is whatever the notes support, including the opposite of the
expected answer.

Group the reasons: not nine problems in a run, but three categories, poor
finances, poor labor relations, and poor public relations.

## Step 5: Plan

The skeleton plan's order is the order the first paragraph announces. Place
each note under a point or cut it; never carry all notes into the draft to
trim later.

A divided point divides into at least two parts. A section with a single
sub-point means the division was wrong; fold it back up.
