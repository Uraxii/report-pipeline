# Evidence

## Comparison

Give every figure a comparison: a prior period, a rival, a target, a total,
or an average.

"Revenue was $4.2 million" -> "Revenue was $4.2 million, up from $3.1 million
the year before and just short of the $4.5 million target."

## Graphs

- A graph makes one point, obvious at first glance. Nothing leaps out -> cut
  the graph.
- Title it with that point, never its topic: "Revenue growth stalled in Q3",
  not "Quarterly Revenue".
- Label every line, axis, bar, and scale.
- Plot two or three variables at most.
- Place it right after the text that discusses it, on the same page where
  possible.
- Line for a trend over a continuous scale. Bar for comparing discrete
  things. Pie only for parts of a known whole, never for time or change.

## Footnotes

Document the startling and the contested, anything whose source reliability
is in question, and quotations, paraphrases, borrowed ideas, and facts that
are not common knowledge. In a memo or short report, use an in-text allusion
or one note at the end; use real footnotes only in a long formal report with
many attributions. Footnote as seldom as the job allows.

## Source credibility

Use several kinds of source, not several sources of one kind. Weigh each by
what it would and would not volunteer:

- A company's own website is advertising. Never rest a claim on it.
- Self-reported biography is not verification.
- Public disclosure filings carry what the subject would not volunteer, such
  as pending lawsuits and audited figures. Weight them accordingly.
- Subjective matters, such as management quality or how a firm treats its
  people, come from periodicals, not from the subject.

## Argument structure

Every supported claim has four parts: claim, evidence, warrant (the reasoning
that links evidence to claim), and qualifier (the conditions under which the
claim holds). State the warrant.

"Turnover is 30 percent, so morale is poor" leaves the warrant unsaid. Write:
"Turnover is 30 percent, triple the sector's 10 percent, and exit interviews
name management in two of every three cases; morale is a likelier cause than
the labor market."

Evidence must pass all five tests, not a majority: authoritative, accurate,
precise, relevant to this claim, and sufficient for the weight placed on it.
One anecdote can pass the first four and still fail on sufficient.

Before sending, check for these fallacies, stacked evidence first:

- Stacked evidence: only what supports the thesis. Fix: state what cuts
  against it.
- Hasty generalization: a rule drawn from too few cases.
- False cause or single cause: one cause named for an effect with several, or
  correlation read as cause.
- Non sequitur: the conclusion does not follow from the facts given.
- Faulty analogy: two things treated as alike when they are not.
- False dilemma: two options offered when more exist.
- Slippery slope: a small step asserted to force an inevitable bad end.
- False authority or "primacy of print": true because someone eminent said
  so, or because it was printed.

## Opinions

Adjudicate every opinion before it enters the report. Accept it as fact only
when it is verifiable and you verified it; include it labeled as someone's
judgment; or reject it.

## Citations

A house citation style supplied for the report wins over this default.

Per-claim citation, inline or as a footnote:

    <author or issuing body>, <title>, <date>, <locator: page, section, or
    URL>. <one-clause descriptor: what kind of source, and its known slant>.

    Acme Corp, Form 10-K FY2024, filed 2025-02-14, p. 47. Audited filing;
    carries litigation the company would not otherwise disclose.

End-of-report source summary, one row per source, assessing each source
against the judgments it supports:

    <source> | <kind> | <strength for this report> | <weakness>

An informal report needs only a list of sources at the end. A formal one
carries both the per-claim citations and the source summary.
