# Style: sentences, numbers, and uncertainty

## The sentence layer

- Active verbs over be-verbs: "I am in favor of" -> "I favor".
- Kill "-tion" nominalizations: "reach a decision" -> "decide".
- Kill "there is" and "there are": "There are three risks that block this"
  -> "Three risks block this".
- Specific words over general: "affected several areas" -> "cut two
  departments and closed one plant".
- Simple common words over Latinate ones: "utilize" -> "use".
- Parallel form in every series: "cheap, fast, and it will work" -> "cheap,
  fast, and reliable".
- Put "only" and "not" beside the word they modify: "He only lost two
  accounts" -> "He lost only two accounts".
- "this", "that", and "which" point at a named thing: "This shows the risk"
  -> "This drop in margin shows the risk".
- Do not overqualify: "I would agree that" -> "I agree".
- No cliches. No slashes for alternatives: write "or" or "and".
- Vary sentence length. Open some sentences with a verb, an adverb, or an
  introductory phrase.
- Take no side on data plural. Route around it: write "statistics are" and
  "information is", never "data is" or "data are".
- One pronoun per company, "it" or "they", held throughout the report.
- Positive form; "not" for denial, never for evasion: "not honest" ->
  "dishonest".
- Put the emphatic words at the end of the sentence.
- Use "very" sparingly: "very good" -> "excellent".
- Cut wordy phrases: "owing to the fact that" -> "since".

Passive voice: the actor comes after the verb, or a form of "to be" sits with
a main verb usually ending in -ed or -en. Cure it by putting the actor before
the verb, dropping part of the verb ("are listed in" -> "are in"), or
changing the verb ("has not been received" -> "has not arrived"). Passive is
allowed only where the actor is unknown or does not matter.

Aim for an average near 17 words a sentence. Split any sentence over 20.

## Numbers and tables

- Words for one through nine, figures for 10 and up.
- Never open a sentence with a figure.
- Write "0.2", never ".2".
- "from" takes "to": "from 2002 to 2008", never "from 2002-2008".
- A table caption sits above the table and states its point: "Table 1:
  Option B costs 40 percent less than Option A", not "Table 1: Costs".
- Left-align text columns. Right-align numeric columns, on the decimal point
  where there is one.
- One header row. No vertical rules. At most one horizontal rule, under the
  header.
- A table resting on assumptions carries the caveat in its own caption.

## The likelihood ladder

Reproduce this table in the report:

| Likelihood         | Probability |
|--------------------|-------------|
| Almost no chance   | 01-05%      |
| Very unlikely      | 05-20%      |
| Unlikely           | 20-45%      |
| Roughly even chance| 45-55%      |
| Likely             | 55-80%      |
| Very likely        | 80-95%      |
| Almost certain(ly) | 95-99%      |

A second word row marks the same seven bands: "Remote | Highly improbable |
Improbable (improbably) | Roughly even odds | Probable (probably) | Highly
probable | Nearly certain". Never mix rows within one judgment. A report that
mixes them anyway carries a disclaimer that the terms mark the same
probability.

State the residual where it would change a decision: "unlikely" still leaves
roughly a one-in-four chance, and "almost certainly not" about five percent.

"High confidence that it is very likely" is two claims in one sentence:
split likelihood and confidence.

## The weasel list

Empty hedging is banned; calibrated probability is mandatory. Replace each
weasel with a ladder term or a plain source attribution: "there is a serious
possibility of default" -> "default is roughly an even chance (45 to 55
percent)".

- Odds words that commit to nothing: "serious possibility", "distinct
  possibility", "just possible", "may well", "doubtless".
- Never modify "possible". Bare "possible" is only for a thing that is neither
  certain nor impossible and cannot be pinned tighter.
- Source-blurring words: "apparently", "seemingly", "appears", "seems",
  "suggests", "indicates", "presumably", "ostensibly", and bare "reportedly".
  Never near a key estimate.
- One probability marker per judgment: "We believe it is likely" stacks two.

When a thing genuinely cannot be estimated, do not refuse and do not weasel:
state the contingencies the answer hangs on, give ranges instead of point
figures, and label the output as assumptions for planning.

## Connectives

The connective sweep looks for the three places a tagword is missing: where a
paragraph turns ("but"), where it gives an example ("for example"), and where
it adds to a list ("also").
