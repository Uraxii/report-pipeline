# Structure: shape of the whole report

## Subheads

A subhead states the segment's sub-conclusion, not its topic:
`Mediocre Performance`, not `Financial Performance`.

## Internal versus external

- Report stays inside the organization -> no cover letter: the thesis
  paragraph leads straight into subheaded segments, with a plain list of
  sources at the end.
- Report leaves the organization -> the same body under a formal cover
  letter.

## The paragraph template

Each paragraph makes one point, distinct from its neighbours, in four moves:

1. **Say it.** State the point in the first sentence, pitched so an
   unspecialized reader grasps it. Carry the technical load later.
2. **Explain it.** Say what the point means and how you will show it.
3. **Detail it.** Give the specifics, the numbers, the documentation.
4. **Say it again.** Restate the point with the detail in perspective, never
   as a copy of the first sentence; it may hand off to the next point.

Opens "Labor relations are poor," closes "Three strikes in two years have
cost more than the wage demands would have, which is why the next section
turns to the balance sheet," not "So labor relations are poor."

## The six paper formats

- Point paper: one page, terse bullets, single issue, minimal data; lets
  someone speak on the issue without notice.
- Talking paper: one page, bullets, single issue; speaking notes that stand
  alone, carried into a planned presentation or briefing.
- Bullet background paper: one or several related issues, one or more pages,
  bullet statements in a concise chronology; summarizes an attached package.
- Background paper: multi-page, full sentences, numbered paragraphs, prose in
  a detailed chronology.
- Position paper: multi-page, full sentences, numbered paragraphs, prose that
  argues one proposal.
- Staff study: multi-page research paper in prose, discussion carried to
  conclusions and recommendations.

## The external cover letter

- Purpose in the first sentence. No preliminary pleasantries.
- Everything the reader must have (times, dates, places, prices, payment
  instructions), and no detail beyond that.
- Specific dates, never "today" or "tomorrow".
- Exactly what response is expected, or what steps are to be taken, when,
  and by whom: "Sign and return the enclosed authorization by March 14 so the
  audit can begin the following week", not "Please respond as soon as
  possible".

## Executive summary

A report long enough to open with a summary gets an executive summary: the
first paragraph's promise expanded, nothing more. It states the thesis and
the main conclusion or recommendation; gives the supporting reasons grouped
in the categories and order the body follows; states the recommended action,
actor, and date; and obeys every exclusion.

Invent no table-of-contents or abstract standard. One needed anyway -> it too
states its point and keeps the announced order.
